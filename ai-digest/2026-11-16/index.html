AI Agents Just Hit a Wall. Here's What Still Works.

Your Weekly Blueprint for AI, Capital & Hustle

Week of November 9–15, 2026 · By Davette Anderson · 6-minute read

You were told AI agents would run your errands for you. This week, a major airline locked them out.

That is the pattern to watch right now. AI tools keep getting better at making things. But the websites, banks, and stores you depend on are deciding how much they will let AI do. If your plan depends on a door someone else controls, they can close it.

1 · The Big Story

United tells AI agents: you can't book here

What happened: On November 11, United Airlines said it is restricting personal AI agents that are not approved by the airline. The limits cover United's own website and app. They also cover travel sites that sell United flights. Travel news site Skift reports United is the first U.S. airline to say this outright.

Why United says it did it:

  • Handing your login to an AI agent can expose private account details.
  • An AI tool could buy something, change your account, or spend your miles by mistake.
  • Some steps need a real person. At check-in, you have to confirm your bags hold nothing banned.

United did not give a start date in the report. It said it will keep reviewing the policy as AI assistants change.

It is not just airlines: Earlier this year, eBay changed its user agreement to bar "buy-for-me" AI agents that eBay has not approved.

Why it matters for your money

Six weeks ago we covered always-on AI agents. The promise was simple: tell the AI the goal and walk away. This week shows the catch. An agent is only as useful as the sites that let it in.

If you sell a service that depends on an AI tool logging in to someone else's website, that site can shut it off without warning. And if you give an agent your password, your card, your points, and your account are all on the line.

What this means for you

  • Business owners: Use AI agents inside tools you control, like your own documents, inbox, and store. Read a site's terms before you automate anything on it.
  • Creators: Do not build a paid offer on a loophole. If a platform has not said yes to AI agents, plan as if the answer is no.
  • Investors: Watch for official partnerships between AI companies and big brands. Those tell you more than a demo video does.

The SHA takeaway: Build on what you control. Borrowed access can be taken back.

2 · Top Upgrades & Tool Releases

Three things worth knowing before you click

Claude Sheets and Whiteboards (Anthropic) — spreadsheets built in the chat

Released November 10 as a beta on paid Claude plans. Sheets builds simple spreadsheets, like a campaign tracker or a budget, with tabs, formulas, and dropdowns. You can export to Excel (.xlsx) or CSV. Whiteboards sorts a pile of notes into themes and drafts action items.

Try this: Ask for a simple launch budget tracker. Then check two rows by hand with a calculator. A formula that looks right can still be wrong. Note: the reports we read did not say the free plan gets these tools.

Shopping with AI? Check the store first

A brand-protection firm called Podqi tested AI shopping answers, as reported by The Neuron on November 13. It found risky or fake store websites in 15% of Perplexity answers, 8% of ChatGPT answers, and 4% of Gemini answers. This is one company's test, not a final score. But holiday shopping is here, so it matters now.

Try this: Before you buy from a store an AI suggested, type the store's web address yourself. Do not tap the link. If you sell products, ask an AI tool where to buy your product and see if a copycat site shows up.

Scam watch: the voice on the phone may not be your boss

In early November, U.S. prosecutors in Manhattan charged three men in an alleged $129 million scheme. Prosecutors say callers used AI to copy the voices or faces of company leaders. A fake "CEO" asked a worker for help with a secret deal. Then a fake "lawyer" sent bank details for a wire transfer. Reported victims include hospital systems, a bank, and a pet food company. These are charges, not convictions.

Try this: Set one rule today so a voice alone can never move your money. The action step below gives you the wording.

3 · Capital & Investments

The Money Trail: where the money is moving

Nvidia's founder steps down as CEO

On November 12, Nvidia said Jensen Huang resigned as president, CEO, and board member, effective right away. Longtime finance chief Colette Kress is interim CEO while the board looks for a permanent one. Nvidia's announcement gave no reason. CNBC reports the exit followed news stories about his personal relationships and a board investigation.

Nvidia makes the chips most AI tools run on. It reports earnings on Tuesday, November 17. CNBC also reports two large shareholders asked the board to take its time with the search.

Investor takeaway: If you own a broad index fund, you likely own some Nvidia. One headline is not a reason to buy or sell. Watch the earnings report and who gets the permanent job.

Founder takeaway: Even a giant company gets shaky when it leans on one person. Write down how your business runs so it does not live only in your head.

Anthropic sets an IPO price range of $70 to $78 a share

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI tools, updated its IPO filing on November 10. It lists a price range of $70 to $78 per share on the Nasdaq under the symbol ANT. Pricing is expected around November 18, with trading as soon as November 19. Those dates can change, and the filing was not yet final when it was reported.

News reports put the company's value between about $1 trillion and $1.4 trillion. The gap comes from how you count the shares.

Investor takeaway: Big-name IPOs can swing hard in the first days. A product you like is not the same as a fair price. Read the risk section of the filing before you put in a dollar.

5 · Weekly Action Step

Write your call-back rule in 15 minutes

A copied voice works because people act fast. A written rule slows things down. Paste this prompt into an AI tool you already use. No new subscription needed.

Act as my operations helper. Write a one-page "money request rule" for my [type of business or household]. The people who can approve payments are: [names or roles]. The rule must say: 1. Any request to send money, change bank details, buy gift cards, or share a login must be confirmed by calling back a phone number we already had on file. Never use the number in the message or the call. 2. We use a safe word that is never sent by text or email. 3. Any new payee over $[amount] waits 24 hours before we pay. 4. "It's urgent" and "keep this secret" are warning signs, not reasons to hurry. Use plain words at an 8th-grade reading level. End with a 5-line checklist I can tape near my desk. Mark anything you are unsure about as [NEEDS INPUT].

Track three things:

  • 📝 Rule written and saved
  • 👥 Who you shared it with
  • 📞 One practice call-back done

Share it with anyone who can touch your money: a partner, a family member, a helper. A rule only works if everyone knows it before the call comes.
